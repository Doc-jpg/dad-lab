# The Happy Body (Aniela and Jerzy Gregorek) — research notes for an original daily-practice app

Researched 2026-11-07 from public sources only (publisher site, FAQ and blog at thehappybody.com, interviews and podcast transcripts, press articles, reader notes and reviews, YouTube/Vimeo oEmbed). Nothing here is quoted from the book. Everything is paraphrased structure and principle. Where a detail is only in the book, it says "not found" or "book only."

Confidence key: [official] = thehappybody.com or the authors speaking; [press] = journalist article or book listing; [reader] = a reader's or reviewer's notes, unverified against the second edition; [conflict] = sources disagree.

## 0. Book and edition facts

- Title: The Happy Body: The Simple Science of Nutrition, Exercise, and Relaxation. Authors Aniela Gregorek and Jerzy Gregorek (former weightlifting champions, Woodside, California). Publisher: Jurania Press. First edition 2009/2010, about 288 pages. [press: archive.org listing]
- Second edition shown on thehappybody.com with a 2020 cover; the second-edition description says it adds new testimonials, a 7-week Quick Guide summary, online resources, and expanded exercise and recipe material. [official site + Goodreads listing]
- The publisher's own promise: a total program (food, exercise, relaxation) that teaches you to lose about 1.0 to 2.5 pounds per week safely and keep it off, while restoring flexibility and posture and getting leaner, stronger and faster. [press: book listing]
- Companion materials: a four-disc DVD set (now sold as digital videos, $99, delivered as private YouTube links), an exercise poster, and later books (Mastering Food Choices, Mastering Exercise Choices, a Self-Mastery Workbook). [official purchase page; Tim Ferriss show notes]
- Official YouTube channel: "The Happy Body" (https://www.youtube.com/@thehappybody, channel id UCS5Nf1BxwzwdqdS1ccN8_bg). Confirmed via oEmbed author_name "The Happy Body". Official Vimeo account "Jerzy Gregorek" hosts the free Sequence 1 Level 1 video.

## 1. Core promise and the qualities it targets

The program is framed as reversing "aging" markers rather than dieting. The site's thesis: endurance cardio burns muscle and leads to exhaustion and injury; calorie counting and strict restriction do not stick; nobody schedules relaxation, which is what lets the body burn fat. So: light daily resistance work done mindfully, a fixed eating schedule, and deliberate relaxation. [official program page]

Six standards (the cover lists five words; the authors count six because "ideal body weight" and "lean" are separate standards): [official]

1. Flexibility (ability of muscles and tendons to lengthen and shorten; lost through lifestyle, not age)
2. Strength (ability to produce force; reference movement is the Overhead Squat Press)
3. Speed (quick brain-nerve-muscle reaction; protects against falls; also tested on the Overhead Squat Press, only after strength standard is reached)
4. Leanness (fat-to-muscle ratio; "Ideal Body Proportion")
5. Good posture (aligned body moves without pain; loss of height is called the first sign of lost posture)
6. Ideal body weight (the scale number for your height and type; 13 body types are described)

Every standard is meant to be "100% measurable" with five grades: Poor, Fair, Good, Very Good, Excellent. [official + reader notes]

The site also describes a 7-week guided arc: measure where you are, follow a plan to improve, do a progress check, then switch to a maintenance plan. [official home page]

A key mindset principle repeated everywhere: micro-progression. Change by very small increments (Jerzy talks about inch-by-inch, half-inch, even quarter-inch range gains, and always keeping a 1 to 2 percent reserve). "Hard choices, easy life" is the motto. [Tim Ferriss transcript]

## 2. The daily exercise sequence

### Shape of the routine
- 18 exercises, organized as 3 sequences of 6 exercises. Sequence 1 is the easiest; sequences 2 and 3 build on it. [official FAQ, site, Tim Ferriss transcript]
- Each exercise has 5 levels of difficulty (level 1 is a simplified version using only some of the movement steps; level 5 is the full movement). [official; reader notes]
- Done every day, like brushing your teeth. Target duration: about 30 minutes for the full 18 once learned. Beginners take longer and work to get faster. The early press coverage (2009) said 40 minutes; the current site and FAQ say 30. Sequence 1 Level 1 alone is listed as 15 to 20 minutes while learning, and the official demo video of it runs 6 minutes 11 seconds at the authors' pace. [official FAQ/site; press] [conflict on 30 vs 40 minutes: the FAQ's 30 is current]
- You may split it into two halves (morning/evening) or even into short 5-minute pieces across the day. If you run out of time, stop at 30 minutes and pick up fresh tomorrow, or finish it. [official FAQ]
- Movement families: pressing, pulling, squatting, bending. No endurance work (running, cycling) is part of the program. [press; official]

### Exercise names (as publicly known)
Full list of 18 names: not found publicly. Known names:
- Sequence 1 (from a reader's notes on the book, in order): Standing Tall; Cresting Wave; Take Off; Tapping Hammer; Sitting Tower (a squat with a dumbbell held under the chin); exercise 6 is listed in those notes also as "Tapping Hammer" with a wide stance and toes out 45 degrees, which looks like a transcription error. [reader; treat as approximate]
- Exercise 2.4 is "Rocking Trunk" (official video preview label). [official]
- "Power Tower" = the Overhead Squat Press, the program's key exercise and its strength standard. The official blog places it as Sequence 3, exercise 5; in the Tim Ferriss transcript Jerzy calls the power tower exercise 5 of sequence 1 and "the most difficult of the 18." [conflict; the written blog post is probably the more careful source. Possibly a simpler arms-crossed version sits in sequence 1 and the full version in sequence 3.]
- "Candle" / "Candle Squat" / "Jerzy Squat": a flexibility test movement (deep squat with arms overhead, stick or bar), not one of the 18 daily exercises. Other flexibility test positions are named The Table, The Jackknife, The Bow, The Corkscrew. [reader notes; official video titles]

### Reps, sets, tempo
- Working loads are chosen so you can do about 6 comfortable repetitions of an exercise. The reader notes on week one also show 6 reps per exercise, with each rep timed (roughly 6 to 11 seconds per rep depending on the exercise, so about 36 to 66 seconds per exercise). [official blog comment; reader]
- Rounds: unclear. The reader notes say "six sets of six reps" for week one, but that may be a misreading of the six-beat rep structure (below). Jerzy describes athletes doing "one or two rounds" of the sequence. A one-pass reading (each of 18 exercises done once for 6 reps) is consistent with a 30-minute total; a six-round reading is not. [conflict, flag for the app: make rounds configurable, default 1]
- Speed is gained by spending less time in the movement and more time resting between reps, not by rushing the movement. [reader]
- Never get out of breath. If you do, slow down, lighten the load, or drop a level and rest until comfortable. No sweating-as-a-goal. [official FAQ; blog "No Sweat"]
- No caffeine before the routine (it fights the parasympathetic state the routine is built on). [official FAQ]

### Breathing (the "six beats" of every repetition) [official site page for Sequence 1 Level 1; Active Meditation blog; Tim Ferriss transcript]
1. Inhale (expand the rib cage, shoulders down and back, head neutral)
2. Tighten / brace (stomach and glutes)
3. Lift (the movement, breath held)
4. Stretch / reach (the end position)
5. Return / lower
6. Exhale and release

Older press describes the same thing in three parts: inhale, move while holding the breath, exhale at the end. The authors call this "active meditation": a mantra-like cycle that keeps attention on one action at a time and keeps the body parasympathetic during exercise. A form cue repeated in press: curl the toes up during lifts so the weight sits on the heels.

### Equipment
- Light dumbbells and a mat or towel; a small space at home. Press coverage gives starting dumbbells of about 3 lb for women and 5 lb for men; the DVD review says 1 to 3 lb for women and 5 to 8 lb for men; one interview says start at 1 lb if you have never lifted. [press] [conflict: all point to "very light"]
- Traveling: water bottles, cans, fillable "aquabells," or no weight at all with full attention to form. [official FAQ]
- Later, Olympic-style equipment (bar, Indo balance board) appears in advanced practice, but is not part of the 18. [transcript]

### How progression works
- Weights for every exercise are set as a percentage of one dumbbell's weight in Exercise 1 of Sequence 1, whether the exercise uses one or two dumbbells. Reader notes for Sequence 1 show percentages of 100%, none, none, 150%, 200%, 150% for exercises 1 through 6, which fits that rule (exercises 2 and 3 are bodyweight). The full percentage table is book-only (Table 8.3). [official FAQ; reader]
- Do not add weight to exercises that feel easy; keep all 18 in balance. Priority order for progress: (1) move up a level, (2) get faster until the whole thing fits in 30 minutes, (3) only then add weight. If you finish under 30 minutes, go up a level; at the top level, add weight. [official FAQ]
- Move up a level when weights feel light and there is no tightness or soreness. Progression speed is individual: monthly to yearly. [official FAQ]
- Micro-increments: the FAQ's concrete example is depth, not load: start squats 1 to 2 inches deep and go 1 inch lower after at least two weeks without soreness; use a box or stacked paper or a plank under the heels to measure small depth steps. Jerzy's cerebral-palsy client progressed 3 → 6 → 10 → 15 → 18 lb before bigger jumps. No official "quarter-pound" rule found; the principle is "smallest possible step, always with a reserve." [official FAQ; official blog; transcript]
- If flexibility is the limit, keep weight and work depth; if strength is the limit, keep depth and add weight gradually. Form first; working above your level reinforces asymmetries. "No pain = gain." [official blog]
- Once you reach the strength and speed standards, switch to maintenance: stop increasing speed or weight (injury risk). [official FAQ]
- Retest the five measures (book pages 16 to 20) about every six weeks; if no progress, review form. [official blog]
- The 58% (men) / 45% (women) of bodyweight figure is a single-rep standard for the Overhead Squat Press, used to track progress, not a daily training weight. Example from a reader: a 42 kg max meant training at about 15 kg for 6 reps. [official blog]

## 3. Nutrition approach (principles only)

- Structure: 2 meals and 3 snacks, every day at the same times, about 3 hours apart counted from the start of eating, inside roughly a 12-hour window. Ideal spacing has about 6 hours between the two meals. Breakfast can be one of the meals or one of the snacks. Example day: snack 9 am, meal 12, snack 3 pm, meal 6 pm, snack 9 pm. [official FAQ; official blog "Mastering Timing"]
- Why 3 hours: about two hours of digestion/nourishment, then the body draws on fat during the third hour. [official blog]
- Eat the scheduled snack even if not hungry; skipping leads to overeating later. [official FAQ]
- Meal composition: one protein plus one or two vegetables. Vegetables about twice the protein by weight. Macro target stated in the FAQ as roughly 15/70/15 protein/carbohydrate/fat. Pasta or pizza allowed if treated as the "protein" and paired with double their weight in vegetables. [official FAQ; press]
- Portion sizing is tied to your ideal body weight, not calorie counting. The protein portion at a meal is sized by total food weight (for example, a 120-lb ideal weight means the salmon portion is measured as total grams of salmon, not grams of protein). Reader notes describe a hand guide: to lose, about one handful protein and two of vegetables; to maintain, one and a half and three; to gain, two and four. [official FAQ; reader] The exact gram/calorie tables are book-only.
- Snacks: whole foods (yogurt, fruit, nuts, vegetable juice, homemade bars). Bars are a transition tool, fine in a pinch. Limit salt; adjust water to the water in your food; a glass of water with each snack. [official FAQ; reader]
- No calorie counting, but a food journal at the start. Quality over quantity (fewer, better ingredients). Processed food and cocktails are discouraged; "balance not deprivation." [press]
- Weekly loss target: publisher says 1.0 to 2.5 lb per week. An official blog example works out to 1.65 lb per week (189 → 165 lb in 14.5 weeks at a deficit of about 825 calories per day, eating about 1,155 calories). A reader report shows a European mentor setting about 500 g per week. So the program's design range is roughly 1 to 2.5 lb per week, with the rate set by body type. [press; official blog; reader]
- Reaching goal: once at ideal weight, increase snack calories by 50% and watch two weeks; if still losing, increase meal calories by 50%. A weekly "banquet" (one free meal/day) is allowed once at goal, tapering over time. [official FAQ; reader]
- Eating around exercise: not found as a rule. Only related items: no caffeine before the routine; if you feel constantly hungry, suspect endurance exercise, doing the routine too fast, or stress. [official FAQ]
- Alcohol: when drinking, pair with protein only and avoid sweet mixers. [reader]
- Slips: don't dwell, refocus on the next three-hour block. [official blog]

## 4. Relaxation / meditation component

- What: lie down after the routine, eyes closed, attention inward, for about 5 minutes. Same music every time (the authors use Massenet's "Meditation" from Thaïs) and the same scent (lavender oil, sprayed or diffused), so the cue itself trains the body to drop into the parasympathetic state fast. After enough repetition, hearing the music alone calms you. Repeat if you want more. [official FAQ; transcript; press]
- When: at least once daily, immediately after exercise (also used as the program's "daily stress-release technique"). [official; press]
- Why (their framing): exercise plus the breath-hold cycle sits you in a calm state during the workout; the lie-down finishes the shift from sympathetic to parasympathetic so you do not leave the session wired; they say fat burns while rested and asleep, so relaxation and sleep quality are part of the fat-loss design. [official]
- Also counted as relaxation in moderation: baths, massage, soft music, formal meditation. Signs you need more: anxiety, restlessness, irritability. [official FAQ]
- The workout itself is called "active meditation" (section 2). [official blog]

## 5. Measurement and tracking

- Starting assessment, then weekly progress across the six standards; formal retest of the five physical measures about every six weeks. [official home page; official blog]
- Scale: the scale is one sign among several (also: heavier weights, easier toe touch, wanting to exercise, preferring vegetables). A weekly weigh-in cadence is implied by the "1 to 2.5 lb per week" and "monitor for two weeks" language but not spelled out. [official blog; official FAQ] Not found: a stated weigh-in day or rule.
- Body fat: the leanness standard is about 10% for men and 13% for women, graded Poor to Excellent (reader notes put "Poor" at 20%+ men / 26%+ women). Method of measurement not specified publicly (readers mention DEXA and body-composition scans). [reader; official program page gives client ranges of 35 to 45% at start]
- Flexibility tests: five positions named The Table, The Jackknife, The Bow, The Corkscrew, The Jerzy (Candle) Squat, each graded Poor to Excellent by how close you get to the full position. Criteria are book-only. [reader]
- Strength test: one Overhead Squat Press with two dumbbells totaling 58% of bodyweight (men) or 45% (women) = the standard (100%); grades scale by percentage of that standard (reader notes: "Poor" under about 25% of it). [official blog; reader]
- Speed test: time to perform the Overhead Squat Press at standard load; reader notes give Excellent at 5 seconds or less and Poor at 12 seconds or more. Only tested after the strength standard is met. [reader; official]
- Posture test: stand with back to a wall, arms straight overhead; grade by which points touch the wall (heels and buttocks only = Poor; add shoulder blades = Fair; add back of head = Good; add backs of hands = Very Good; add elbows = Excellent). A side photo with a vertical line from ankle through hip, shoulder and ear is the visual check. [reader]
- Ideal body weight: a height table, by sex, not a formula with wrist size. Reader notes show the table rising about 3 lb per inch for women and 5 lb per inch for men (for example 104/120 lb at 5'0", 134/170 lb at 5'10", 140/180 at 6'0"). Grading is by how many "steps" you are from ideal (5 steps = Poor, 1 step = Excellent). Treat as approximate; verify against the second edition before coding. [reader]
- Ideal Body Proportion (IBP): the ratio of body-fat percentage to muscle-mass percentage; the program distinguishes IBW (scale number) from IBP (composition), and rejects BMI because it ignores sex and fat vs muscle. [official blog "Decoding BMI vs IBW & IBP"]
- 13 body types, defined by combinations of weight, fat and muscle relative to ideal (reader notes list names such as Happy Body, Gymnast, Scale Watcher, Lineman, Gym Member, Sumo Wrestler, The Sitter, The Bodybuilder, The Fashion Model, The Twig, The Dieter, The Dancer, The Mountain Climber), each with its own eating and training emphasis (types 4 to 8 lose weight; 9 to 13 gain). Book pages 83 to 96. [reader; official blog reference]
- Daily self-check: how you feel on waking. Eager for the day = on track; no energy = you need more rest. [reader]

## 6. Missed days and low-energy days

- No explicit "missed day" protocol found. The closest official guidance: get back on schedule rather than trying to make up lost time; don't dwell on a slip, focus on the next three-hour block (food) or the next day (exercise). Daily consistency is framed as hygiene ("do you brush your teeth every day?"). [official FAQ; official blog; press]
- Short on time: stop at 30 minutes and restart tomorrow, or split into two halves, or do 5-minute pieces through the day. [official FAQ]
- Tired: if the fatigue is from physical work, rest; if it is from travel or sitting, the routine should refresh you. Jet lag: do the routine; sleep quality matters more than hours; oversleeping causes grogginess. [official FAQ]
- Pain: if an exercise hurts even at level 1, skip it, recover, try again later. Never train out of breath. Slow down when you catch yourself speeding up. [official FAQ; transcript]
- Illness or injury: not found.
- Travel: substitute household weights or do the movements unloaded with full form. [official FAQ]
